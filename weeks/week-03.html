Cells & Membranes

You are made of roughly 30 trillion cells, each a busy, walled city. This week we tour the city's districts and learn how its border, the membrane, decides what gets in and out.

1. The cell theory

In 1665 Robert Hooke peered at cork through a crude microscope and saw tiny boxes he called "cells." Shortly after, Antonie van Leeuwenhoek saw living "animalcules" (bacteria and protists) swimming in pond water and dental plaque. By the mid-1800s, Schleiden, Schwann and Virchow had pulled the evidence together into cell theory:

  1. All living things are made of one or more cells.
  2. The cell is the basic unit of structure and function in living things.
  3. All cells come from pre-existing cells.

The third principle (Virchow's omnis cellula e cellula) has a profound implication: every cell in your body descends, through an unbroken chain of divisions, from a single fertilized egg, and ultimately from the first cells on Earth ~3.5–4 billion years ago.

Most cells are microscopic: a typical human cell is 10–30 micrometers (µm) across, and a bacterium 1–2 µm. Why so small? As a cell grows, its volume increases faster than its surface area. Since everything the cell needs must cross its surface, a cell that's too big can't supply its interior. You'll explore this in Lab 3C.

🫀 Human connection: you are outnumbered

Your body hosts roughly as many bacterial cells as human cells, about 38 trillion, mostly in your gut. Because bacteria are so small, they weigh only about 0.2 kg in total. These microbes help digest food, make vitamin K and train your immune system (Week 10).

3. A tour of the eukaryotic cell

Think of the eukaryotic cell as a factory city. Use the Cell Explorer in the Virtual Lab alongside this section.

Information headquarters

  • Nucleus: houses DNA and controls which genes are used. Its double-membrane envelope is perforated by nuclear pores that let mRNA out. Inside, the nucleolus builds ribosome parts.

The production line (the endomembrane system)

  • Ribosomes: the protein builders. Free ribosomes make proteins used in the cytoplasm. Ribosomes on the ER make proteins for export or for membranes.
  • Rough ER: folds and modifies new proteins.
  • Smooth ER: makes lipids and steroid hormones, detoxifies drugs (abundant in liver cells), and stores calcium (in muscle).
  • Golgi apparatus: the shipping department. It receives vesicles from the ER, modifies proteins (e.g., adding sugar tags), sorts them and ships them out.
  • Vesicles carry cargo between compartments and to the cell surface.
  • Lysosomes: recycling centers full of digestive enzymes that break down old organelles and engulfed invaders.

Follow a protein hormone like insulin through the system: gene (nucleus) → mRNA → ribosome on rough ER → Golgi → vesicle → released at the membrane.

Power plants

  • Mitochondria carry out cellular respiration, converting food energy into ATP (Week 4). They have a folded inner membrane (cristae) and their own DNA.
  • Chloroplasts (plants and algae) capture light energy through photosynthesis.

Mitochondria and chloroplasts are about the size of bacteria, have their own circular DNA and bacteria-like ribosomes, and divide by splitting. The endosymbiotic theory proposes they were once free-living bacteria that were engulfed by an ancestral cell more than 1.5 billion years ago and never left.

Structure and movement

  • Cytoskeleton: protein fibers that give the cell shape and act as tracks. Microfilaments (actin) drive muscle contraction and cell crawling, microtubules form the spindle during division and the core of cilia and flagella, and intermediate filaments provide tensile strength.
  • Cilia and flagella: cilia in your airways sweep mucus upward, and sperm swim with a flagellum.

Plant extras

Plant cells add a rigid cellulose cell wall, chloroplasts and a large central vacuole that presses outward to keep the plant firm. Note: plant cells also have mitochondria.

White blood cells that eat bacteria would be rich in which organelle?

Lysosomes, the organelles that digest engulfed bacteria.

4. The plasma membrane: a selective border

Every cell is wrapped in a plasma membrane just ~7–10 nanometers thick. It's described by the fluid mosaic model: a fluid bilayer of phospholipids (heads facing water, tails hidden inside) with a mosaic of proteins floating in it. Cholesterol tucked between phospholipids keeps fluidity just right. Membrane proteins act as channels, pumps, receptors for hormones, enzymes and ID tags. Carbohydrate chains on the outer surface (the glycocalyx) act as "name badges": your ABO blood type is one of them.

The membrane is selectively permeable. Small nonpolar molecules (O₂, CO₂) slip right through the oily core. Ions and larger polar molecules (glucose, Na⁺) need protein help.

5. Moving across the membrane

Passive transport (no energy needed)

  • Diffusion: molecules spread from high to low concentration, down their concentration gradient. O₂ diffuses from your lungs into your blood this way.
  • Facilitated diffusion: still downhill, but through channel or carrier proteins (e.g., glucose transporters, water channels called aquaporins).
  • Osmosis: diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane. Water moves toward the side with more dissolved solute (and therefore relatively less free water).

Compared with the inside of a cell, a solution can be:

  • Hypotonic (less solute outside): water rushes in. Animal cells swell and may burst (lyse). Plant cells become firm (turgid), which is healthy for them because the wall resists bursting.
  • Isotonic (equal): no net movement. This is why IV fluids are 0.9% saline.
  • Hypertonic (more solute outside): water leaves. Animal cells shrivel (crenate) and plant cells plasmolyze. Salt-curing meat works because bacteria shrivel in hypertonic salt.

Active transport (requires energy)

Moving something against its gradient costs energy, usually ATP. The star example is the sodium–potassium pump, which pushes 3 Na⁺ out and brings 2 K⁺ in for each ATP. It is estimated to use 20–25% of your resting energy, and much more in the brain, because every nerve impulse depends on the gradients it builds (Week 9).

Bulk transport

Large items move in membrane-wrapped packages. Endocytosis brings material in, as when white blood cells engulf bacteria (phagocytosis) or cells take in cholesterol-carrying LDL particles. Exocytosis releases material, as when cells secrete hormones, neurotransmitters and digestive enzymes.

🫀 Human connection: cystic fibrosis

Cystic fibrosis is caused by mutations in a single membrane channel protein, CFTR, which moves chloride ions out of cells. When it fails, less water follows by osmosis, and the mucus in the lungs and pancreas becomes thick and sticky. Newer drugs called CFTR modulators help the faulty protein fold and open, and have transformed treatment for many patients.

Key takeaways
  • All living things are made of cells, and all cells come from cells.
  • Prokaryotes lack a nucleus and organelles. Eukaryotes have both.
  • Proteins are made at ribosomes, processed in the ER and Golgi, and shipped in vesicles. Mitochondria make ATP.
  • The membrane is a fluid mosaic of lipids and proteins that controls traffic.
  • Passive transport goes down gradients for free. Active transport goes against them using ATP.

Practical: Potato Osmosis

You'll find the salt concentration that's isotonic to potato cells by measuring how much mass potato pieces gain or lose in different salt solutions.

⚠️ SafetyTake care with knives; have an adult help with cutting. Don't eat the samples afterward.

Materials

  • 1–2 potatoes, cut into 5 equal pieces (e.g., 1 × 1 × 4 cm strips, or "fries")
  • Table salt and a measuring spoon
  • 5 cups, each with 200 mL water
  • Kitchen scale (ideally reading to 0.1 g), paper towels, marker

Making solutions

Dissolve salt into 200 mL water: 0 g · 2 g (~⅓ tsp) · 4 g (~⅔ tsp) · 10 g (~1¾ tsp) · 20 g (~3½ tsp). That gives roughly 0%, 1%, 2%, 5% and 10% salt.

Procedure

  1. Pat each potato piece dry, weigh it and record its initial mass.
  2. Place one piece in each solution. Note how firm or floppy it feels.
  3. Leave for at least 2 hours (overnight is better).
  4. Remove, blot gently and the same way each time, and record the final mass. Note the texture.
  5. Calculate: % change = (final − initial) ÷ initial × 100.
  6. The chart below plots your data automatically. Where does the line cross 0% change?

Data table

The first column is the salt % (used as the x-axis). Enter numbers only in the % change column for the chart.

Analysis

Extension: the naked eggSoak a raw egg in vinegar for 1–2 days until the shell dissolves (acid + calcium carbonate → CO₂ bubbles!). Then move it to corn syrup overnight and then to water overnight, measuring its circumference each time. The egg's membrane is a giant model of a cell membrane.
