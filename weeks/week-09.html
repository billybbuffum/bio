The Human Body I: Control Systems

Now we zoom out from cells to you. How do trillions of cells coordinate to keep your temperature, blood sugar and posture just right? This week covers the body's organization and its two great control systems, nerves and hormones, along with the muscles and bones they command.

1. How the body is organized

Your body is built in levels: cells → tissues → organs → organ systems → organism. A tissue is a group of similar cells doing a shared job. Despite the body's complexity, there are only four basic tissue types:

TissueJobExamples
EpithelialCovers, lines, protects, secretes, absorbsSkin's outer layer, lining of gut and lungs, glands
ConnectiveSupports, connects, stores, transportsBone, cartilage, tendons, fat, blood

Organs combine several tissues. Your stomach has epithelium lining it, smooth muscle churning it, connective tissue holding it together and nerves controlling it. Organs work together in organ systems: nervous, endocrine, musculoskeletal, cardiovascular, respiratory, digestive, urinary, immune/lymphatic, integumentary (skin) and reproductive.

2. Homeostasis and feedback

Homeostasis is the maintenance of a stable internal environment despite a changing world. Body temperature stays near 37 °C, blood glucose near 70–100 mg/dL when fasting, and blood pH near 7.4. Most homeostasis uses negative feedback, a loop that reverses a change:

  1. Stimulus: a variable drifts from its set point (body temperature rises).
  2. Sensor/receptor: detects the change (temperature receptors in skin and brain).
  3. Control center: compares the value to the set point and decides on a response (the hypothalamus).
  4. Effector: carries out the response (sweat glands secrete, and skin blood vessels dilate).
  5. Response: temperature falls back toward the set point, and the signal shuts off.

Your home thermostat works the same way. When you're cold, the loop runs in reverse: blood vessels constrict, you shiver (muscle contractions generate heat), and you get goosebumps, a leftover from furrier ancestors.

Positive feedback amplifies a change until some endpoint is reached. It's rarer and always self-limiting. Examples: during childbirth, oxytocin causes contractions, which trigger more oxytocin, until the baby is born. In blood clotting, activated platelets recruit more platelets until the wound is sealed.

3. The nervous system: fast electrical control

Neurons

The basic unit is the neuron. Dendrites receive signals, the cell body integrates them, and a long axon sends the signal on, sometimes over a meter, from your spine to your toes. Supporting glial cells nourish, protect and insulate neurons. Your brain has about 86 billion neurons and a similar number of glia.

Resting potential

A resting neuron is like a charged battery. The Na⁺/K⁺ pump (Week 3) keeps Na⁺ high outside and K⁺ high inside, and the inside sits at about −70 mV relative to the outside.

The action potential

When incoming signals push the membrane to the threshold (about −55 mV), voltage-gated channels open in sequence:

  1. Depolarization: Na⁺ channels open, and Na⁺ rushes in. The inside shoots up to about +30 mV.
  2. Repolarization: Na⁺ channels close and K⁺ channels open. K⁺ flows out and the voltage falls.
  3. Hyperpolarization: it briefly overshoots below −70 mV, then the pump restores the resting state. During this refractory period the neuron can't fire again, so impulses travel in only one direction.

Action potentials are all-or-none: either threshold is reached and a full-size spike fires, or nothing happens. A stronger stimulus makes a neuron fire more often, not more strongly. The spike regenerates itself along the axon. Fatty myelin insulation lets it jump between gaps (nodes of Ranvier), reaching speeds of up to ~120 m/s. In multiple sclerosis, the immune system attacks myelin and signals slow or fail.

Synapses

Neurons don't touch. At a synapse, the arriving action potential causes the axon terminal to release neurotransmitters from vesicles by exocytosis. They diffuse across a ~20 nm gap and bind receptors on the next cell, exciting or inhibiting it. Examples include acetylcholine (muscle contraction), dopamine (reward, movement; lost in Parkinson's disease), serotonin (mood) and GABA (the main inhibitory transmitter). Many drugs act here: SSRI antidepressants block serotonin reuptake, caffeine blocks adenosine receptors, and nicotine mimics acetylcholine.

Organization

  • Central nervous system (CNS): the brain and spinal cord, which integrate information.
  • Peripheral nervous system (PNS): nerves throughout the body. It has a somatic division (voluntary control of skeletal muscle) and an autonomic division (involuntary control). The autonomic division is split into the sympathetic ("fight or flight": faster heart, dilated pupils, digestion paused) and parasympathetic ("rest and digest") systems.
Brain regionMain functions

A reflex arc skips the brain for speed: receptor → sensory neuron → spinal cord interneuron → motor neuron → muscle. That's why you jerk your hand off a hot stove before you feel the pain.

4. The endocrine system: slower chemical control

Hormones are chemical messengers secreted by endocrine glands into the blood. They reach every cell but affect only target cells with the matching receptor. Compared with nerves, hormones act more slowly but their effects last longer. Steroid hormones (made from cholesterol) pass through membranes and alter gene expression directly. Protein hormones bind surface receptors and trigger signaling cascades inside the cell.

  • The hypothalamus and pituitary ("master gland") direct many other glands: thyroid (metabolism), adrenal glands (stress), gonads (reproduction) and growth.
  • Blood sugar: after a meal, rising glucose triggers beta cells in the pancreas to release insulin. Body cells take up glucose, and the liver stores it as glycogen. Between meals, alpha cells release glucagon, and the liver releases glucose. This is a negative feedback pair you'll model in Lab 9C.
  • Stress: the adrenal glands release adrenaline (seconds: racing heart, energy release) and cortisol (hours: raises blood sugar, suppresses inflammation). Chronic high cortisol harms sleep, immunity and blood pressure.
🫀 Human connection: diabetes

Type 1 diabetes is autoimmune: the immune system destroys insulin-producing beta cells, so patients need insulin for life. Type 2 diabetes, about 90–95% of cases, starts with insulin resistance: cells stop responding well, the pancreas overworks, and blood glucose creeps up. It's strongly influenced by genetics, weight, activity and diet, and can often be prevented or managed with exercise, nutrition and medication. Over time, high blood sugar damages blood vessels, nerves, kidneys and eyes.

5. Muscles and bones

The skeleton

An adult has 206 bones. They support the body, protect organs, anchor muscles, store calcium and phosphate, and house red bone marrow, which makes about 2–3 million new red blood cells every second. Bone is living connective tissue: osteoblasts build it and osteoclasts break it down, remodeling about 10% of your skeleton each year. Weight-bearing exercise strengthens bone, and its loss with age (osteoporosis) increases fracture risk. Joints, cartilage, ligaments (bone to bone) and tendons (muscle to bone) enable movement.

Muscle

There are three muscle types: skeletal (voluntary, striped), cardiac (involuntary, striped, only in the heart) and smooth (involuntary, in organ and blood-vessel walls). Skeletal muscle fibers are packed with repeating contractile units called sarcomeres, made of thin actin and thick myosin filaments.

Sliding filament model: a motor neuron releases acetylcholine → the muscle fiber fires an action potential → Ca²⁺ is released inside the cell → Ca²⁺ exposes binding sites on actin → myosin heads grab actin and pull, powered by ATP → filaments slide past each other and the sarcomere shortens. The filaments themselves don't shrink. Muscles can only pull, so they work in opposing pairs (biceps bends the elbow, triceps straightens it). After death, ATP runs out and myosin stays locked onto actin, causing rigor mortis.

Key takeaways
  • There are four tissue types: epithelial, connective, muscle and nervous. Body organization runs cells → tissues → organs → systems.
  • Homeostasis relies mostly on negative feedback (stimulus → sensor → control center → effector → response).
  • Neurons rest at −70 mV and fire all-or-none action potentials (Na⁺ in, then K⁺ out). Synapses use neurotransmitters.
  • Hormones travel in blood to target cells. Insulin and glucagon keep blood sugar balanced.
  • Bone is living and constantly remodeled. Muscles contract as myosin pulls actin, powered by ATP and triggered by Ca²⁺.

Practical: Ruler-Drop Reaction Time & Touch Sensitivity

Part A: Ruler-drop reaction time

Gravity is a perfect timer. A falling ruler accelerates at g = 9.8 m/s², so the distance it falls before you catch it tells you your reaction time: t = √(2d ÷ g), where d is in meters.

  1. Your helper holds a 30 cm ruler vertically, with the 0 cm mark between your open thumb and finger (not touching).
  2. Without warning, they drop it. Catch it as fast as you can, and record the cm mark at the top of your fingers.
  3. Do 5 trials with your dominant hand and 5 with your non-dominant hand.
  4. Convert: e.g., 15 cm = 0.15 m → t = √(0.30 ÷ 9.8) = 0.175 s = 175 ms.

Part B: Two-point discrimination

Your skin's touch receptors aren't evenly spread. Unfold 2 paper clips into U shapes with the tips set at 2 mm, 5 mm, 10 mm, 20 mm and 40 mm apart. With your eyes closed, have your helper touch either one or both tips gently to each body area in random order. Record the smallest separation at which you reliably feel two points.
