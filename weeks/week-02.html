Biomolecules & Enzymes

Four families of large molecules build every living thing. This week you'll learn what they are, what they do in your body, and how enzymes run the whole chemical factory.

Learning objectives

  3. Explain the four levels of protein structure and why shape determines function.
  4. Explain how enzymes lower activation energy.
  5. Predict how temperature, pH, substrate concentration and inhibitors affect enzyme activity.

1. Building big molecules from small ones

Life's large molecules (macromolecules) are built like trains from repeating cars. The small units are monomers and the chains are polymers. Cells use one trick to join them and the reverse trick to take them apart:

  • Dehydration synthesis links two monomers by removing a water molecule (H from one, OH from the other).
  • Hydrolysis ("water-splitting") breaks the bond by adding water back. This is exactly what happens when you digest food.

All four classes are built around carbon and are therefore called organic molecules.

2. Carbohydrates: fuel and framework

Carbohydrates contain C, H and O, usually in a 1:2:1 ratio. Monosaccharides such as glucose, fructose and galactose are single sugars. Two together form a disaccharide: sucrose (table sugar) or lactose (milk sugar). Many together form a polysaccharide:

  • Starch is how plants store glucose. Bread, rice and potatoes are mostly starch.
  • Glycogen is how animals store glucose. It is highly branched for quick release and is stored in your liver and muscles.
  • Cellulose is the structural polymer of plant cell walls and the most abundant organic molecule on Earth. Its glucose units are linked by a different bond (β instead of α) that human enzymes can't break, so it passes through us as dietary fiber.
🫀 Human connection: lactose intolerance

Digesting lactose requires the enzyme lactase. Most mammals, and most of the world's adults, stop making lactase after weaning. Undigested lactose reaches gut bacteria, which ferment it into gas. In some populations with a long history of dairy farming, a mutation keeps lactase switched on into adulthood. It is a striking example of recent human evolution, which you'll revisit in Week 8.

3. Lipids: hydrophobic and essential

Lipids are united not by structure but by a property: they are hydrophobic and don't mix with water.

  • Fats (triglycerides) are a glycerol molecule bonded to three fatty acids. A gram of fat stores about 9 kcal, more than twice the ~4 kcal of carbohydrates or protein, so fat is ideal for long-term storage. Saturated fatty acids have no C=C double bonds, so they are straight and pack tightly; butter is solid. Unsaturated fats have double bonds that kink the chain, so oils are liquid. Artificial trans fats raise heart-disease risk and are now banned in many countries.
  • Phospholipids have two fatty-acid tails (hydrophobic) and a phosphate head (hydrophilic). In water they spontaneously form a bilayer, the basis of every cell membrane.
  • Steroids have four fused carbon rings. Cholesterol keeps membranes at the right fluidity and is the raw material for hormones such as estrogen, testosterone and cortisol, and for vitamin D.

4. Proteins: the molecules that do the work

If DNA is the blueprint, proteins are the workers. Your body makes tens of thousands of different proteins. Each is a chain of amino acids linked by peptide bonds. Every amino acid has the same backbone (an amino group, a carboxyl group and a hydrogen around a central carbon) plus a unique side chain called the R group. The 20 different R groups vary: some are hydrophobic, some charged, some polar. That variety lets proteins take almost any shape.

A protein's shape is described at four levels:

  1. Primary: the sequence of amino acids, dictated by a gene.
  2. Secondary: local coils (α-helices) and folds (β-pleated sheets), held by hydrogen bonds along the backbone.
  3. Tertiary: the overall 3-D shape of one chain, formed as R groups interact. Hydrophobic ones hide inside, and some form disulfide bridges.
  4. Quaternary: several chains assembled together. Hemoglobin has four.

Shape determines function. Change one amino acid and the whole protein can misfold. In sickle-cell disease a single substitution, glutamic acid → valine at one position in hemoglobin, makes the molecules stick together into rods that bend red blood cells into sickles. You'll trace this mutation from DNA to disease in Week 6.

Heat, extreme pH or certain chemicals can disrupt the weak bonds holding a protein's shape. This is denaturation. Cook an egg and the clear albumin protein unfolds and tangles into an opaque white solid. It can't be un-cooked.

Why is a fever above about 41 °C (106 °F) a medical emergency?

At high temperatures, the body's proteins, including the enzymes that run metabolism, begin to denature and stop working. Neurons in the brain are especially sensitive.

5. Nucleic acids: information molecules

DNA and RNA are polymers of nucleotides. Each nucleotide has three parts: a five-carbon sugar, a phosphate group and a nitrogen-containing base. DNA uses the sugar deoxyribose and the bases A, T, G and C. RNA uses ribose and swaps thymine for uracil (U). The sequence of bases encodes genetic information; Weeks 5 and 6 are devoted to how. One nucleotide does double duty: ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is your cells' energy currency (Week 4).

6. Enzymes: life's catalysts

Most chemical reactions in your body would take years to happen on their own at 37 °C. Enzymes, mostly proteins, speed them up by factors of millions. Every reaction needs an initial push called the activation energy. Enzymes lower that barrier without being used up and without changing the reaction's overall energy.

Each enzyme has an active site, a pocket shaped to fit its specific substrate. As the substrate binds, the enzyme shifts slightly to grip it more snugly (induced fit), strains its bonds, and releases the products. The enzyme is then free to grab another. A single catalase molecule can break down millions of hydrogen peroxide molecules per second.

What affects enzyme activity?

  • Temperature. Warmer molecules move faster and collide more, so the rate rises up to an optimum (~37 °C for most human enzymes). Beyond that the enzyme denatures and the rate crashes.
  • pH. Each enzyme has an optimal pH. Pepsin works in the stomach at pH ~2, and trypsin in the small intestine at ~pH 8.
  • Substrate concentration. More substrate means more collisions, until every active site is busy. The enzyme is then saturated, and the rate plateaus.
  • Inhibitors. Competitive inhibitors mimic the substrate and block the active site. Noncompetitive inhibitors bind elsewhere and change the enzyme's shape. Many drugs are enzyme inhibitors: ibuprofen blocks the COX enzymes that make inflammatory signals, and statins block an enzyme in cholesterol synthesis.
  • Cofactors and coenzymes. Many enzymes need helpers: minerals (zinc, iron, magnesium) or organic molecules, often made from vitamins. This is one reason vitamins are essential in your diet.
Key takeaways
  • Monomers join by dehydration synthesis and split by hydrolysis.
  • Carbohydrates provide energy and structure; lipids store energy, form membranes and make hormones; proteins do most of the work; nucleic acids store information.
  • A protein's amino-acid sequence determines its shape, and its shape determines its function.
  • Enzymes lower activation energy and are sensitive to temperature, pH, substrate level and inhibitors.

Practical: Catalase, the Bubbling Enzyme

Your cells constantly produce hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), a toxic by-product of metabolism. The enzyme catalase breaks it down almost instantly: 2 H₂O₂ → 2 H₂O + O₂. Potatoes are rich in catalase. You'll measure the oxygen foam it produces and test how temperature affects it.

⚠️ SafetyUse only 3% (pharmacy-grade) hydrogen peroxide. Avoid contact with eyes; rinse skin with water if splashed. Get an adult's help with boiling water. Don't eat the potato pieces afterward.

Materials

  • 3% hydrogen peroxide (~100 mL)
  • 1 raw potato, cut into 4 equal cubes (~1 cm) or grated equal teaspoons
  • 4 identical narrow glasses, cups or test tubes
  • Ruler, timer, measuring spoon
  • Ice, boiling water, small bowls

Procedure

  1. Prepare four potato samples of equal size:
    A: room temperature (control) · B: in ice water 10 min · C: in warm water (~40 °C) 10 min · D: boiled 5 min, then cooled.
  2. Add 2 tablespoons (30 mL) of peroxide to each glass.
  3. Drop sample A into its glass and start the timer.
  4. After 1 minute, measure the height of the foam in mm.
  5. Repeat for B, C and D. If you can, do 2–3 trials of each and average them.
  6. Extension: try a mashed sample vs. a whole cube (surface area!), or a drop of vinegar added (pH).
