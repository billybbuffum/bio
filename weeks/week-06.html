Gene Expression & Mutation

DNA is a recipe book, but a recipe doesn't feed anyone until it is cooked. This week we follow information from a gene to a working protein, learn how cells choose which recipes to use, and see how a single typo can change a life.

1. The central dogma

Francis Crick summarized the flow of genetic information as the central dogma of molecular biology:

DNA —transcription→ RNA —translation→ Protein

DNA stays safely in the nucleus like a master reference book that never leaves the library. When a protein is needed, the cell makes a disposable working copy of one gene in RNA and sends it out to the protein-building machinery. There are known exceptions: retroviruses such as HIV copy RNA back into DNA using the enzyme reverse transcriptase.

2. Transcription: DNA → RNA

Transcription happens in the nucleus. RNA polymerase binds to a DNA sequence called the promoter just upstream of a gene, unwinds a short section, and reads one strand, the template strand, building a complementary RNA 5'→3'. The other DNA strand is the coding strand, so called because the RNA matches its sequence (with U for T). Transcription stops at a terminator sequence.

Example: template DNA 3'-TAC GGA TTC-5' → mRNA 5'-AUG CCU AAG-3'.

mRNA processing (eukaryotes)

Before leaving the nucleus, the new pre-mRNA is edited:

  • A protective 5' cap and a poly-A tail are added. They help export and protect the mRNA and mark it for translation.
  • Splicing: non-coding sections called introns are cut out, and the coding exons are joined together. Through alternative splicing, the same gene can be spliced in different ways to make different proteins. This is part of how ~20,000 genes produce perhaps 100,000+ different proteins.

3. The genetic code

The mRNA is read in groups of three bases called codons. With 4 bases, there are 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 codons, coding for 20 amino acids plus stop signals. Key features:

  • AUG is the start codon and codes for methionine (Met).
  • UAA, UAG, UGA are stop codons.
  • The code is redundant: most amino acids have several codons (GGU, GGC, GGA and GGG all mean glycine). Often only the third base differs, which buffers against some mutations.
  • The code is unambiguous: each codon means only one thing.
  • The code is nearly universal: bacteria, plants and people read it the same way. This is powerful evidence of common ancestry, and it's why bacteria can make human insulin from a human gene.

4. Translation: RNA → protein

Translation happens at ribosomes in the cytoplasm or on the rough ER. Three kinds of RNA cooperate:

  • mRNA carries the codons.
  • tRNA molecules are adapters. Each carries one specific amino acid at one end and has a three-base anticodon at the other that pairs with a matching codon.
  • rRNA forms the core of the ribosome and actually catalyzes the peptide bonds. The ribosome is a ribozyme, an RNA enzyme.
  1. Initiation: the small ribosomal subunit binds the mRNA and finds AUG. A tRNA carrying Met pairs with it, and the large subunit joins.
  2. Elongation: the next tRNA pairs with the next codon, the ribosome links its amino acid to the growing chain, and then the ribosome shifts one codon along. This repeats at around 5–20 amino acids per second.
  3. Termination: at a stop codon, a release factor frees the finished chain, which folds (often with help) into its working shape.
Translate the mRNA 5'-AUG UGG AAA UAG-3'.

Met – Trp – Lys – stop. The protein is three amino acids long.

🫀 Human connection: mRNA vaccines

The COVID-19 mRNA vaccines use translation directly. They deliver an mRNA encoding the virus's spike protein, wrapped in a lipid bubble. Your ribosomes translate it, your immune system learns to recognize the spike (Week 10), and the mRNA is broken down within days. It never enters the nucleus and cannot change your DNA. Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman won the 2023 Nobel Prize for the modifications that made this possible.

5. Gene regulation: same DNA, different cells

A neuron and a skin cell in your body contain identical DNA. They differ because they express different genes. Cells control gene expression at every step, but the most important control is whether a gene is transcribed at all.

  • Transcription factors are proteins that bind DNA near a gene and switch it on or off. Combinations of them act like a cell's identity code. Adding just four transcription factors can reprogram an adult skin cell into a stem cell (Yamanaka's Nobel-winning discovery of iPS cells, 2012).
  • Chromatin packing: tightly wound DNA can't be read. Chemical tags on histones loosen or tighten it.
  • Epigenetics: marks such as DNA methylation change gene activity without changing the sequence, and they can be passed on when cells divide. Diet, stress and toxins can affect these marks.
  • In bacteria, related genes are often grouped into operons controlled by a single switch. The lac operon turns on lactose-digesting genes only when lactose is present.

6. Mutations: when the code changes

A mutation is any change in the DNA sequence. Mutations arise from rare copying errors and from mutagens such as UV light, tobacco smoke chemicals and radiation. Most are harmless, some are harmful, and a few are beneficial: they are the raw material of evolution.

Point mutations (one base substituted)

  • Silent: the new codon codes for the same amino acid (GGU → GGC, both Gly).
  • Missense: one amino acid is replaced by another. The effect depends on where it is and how different the new amino acid is.
  • Nonsense: a codon becomes a stop codon, producing a truncated, usually non-functional protein.

Insertions and deletions

Adding or removing bases in a number that isn't a multiple of 3 causes a frameshift. Every codon downstream is misread, like removing one letter from the sentence THE BIG CAT ATE THE RAT, which becomes THB IGC ATA TET HER AT. Frameshifts usually destroy the protein.

🩸 Human connection: sickle-cell disease and malaria

Sickle-cell disease is caused by one base change in the β-globin gene: GAG → GTG, which turns glutamic acid (charged) into valine (hydrophobic). People with two copies have painful crises and anemia, because hemoglobin forms rigid rods that deform red cells. But people with one copy are significantly protected against severe malaria. That's why the allele is common where malaria is, or was, widespread, a vivid example of natural selection (Week 8). In 2023, a CRISPR-based therapy for sickle-cell disease became the first approved gene-editing treatment.

Somatic vs. germline: mutations in body cells (somatic) affect only that person and can contribute to cancer when they hit genes controlling cell division. Mutations in eggs or sperm (germline) can be inherited.

Key takeaways
  • DNA → RNA (transcription, in the nucleus) → protein (translation, at ribosomes).
  • Pre-mRNA is capped, tailed and spliced, and introns are removed.
  • Codons are triplets. AUG starts translation and UAA, UAG and UGA stop it. The code is redundant and nearly universal.
  • tRNA anticodons match mRNA codons, and the ribosome links the amino acids.
  • Gene regulation explains how cells differ. Mutations can be silent, missense, nonsense or frameshift.

Practical: Decode the Sickle-Cell Gene by Hand

Real molecular biologists still check sequences by eye. You'll be the RNA polymerase and the ribosome, using only pencil, paper and the codon table, then build a physical model of how one amino acid change alters a protein.

Part A: Transcribe and translate

Below are the template strands for the first 7 codons of the normal and sickle-cell β-globin genes. For each: (1) write the mRNA (5'→3'), (2) split it into codons, (3) translate using the codon table (in the Virtual Lab, or printed). Don't use the simulator until you've finished, then use it to check.

Normal template: 3'-TAC CAC GTG GAC TGA GGA CTC-5'
Sickle template: 3'-TAC CAC GTG GAC TGA GGA CAC-5'

Part B: The secret word

This template strand hides a word in one-letter amino acid codes (the letters after the starting Met): 3'-TAC AGC TAG ACG TTC AAC CTC ATT-5'

Part C: Build a protein model

  1. Take a pipe cleaner or string (the polypeptide backbone) and thread on about 12 beads in two colors: one color for hydrophobic amino acids and another for charged/polar ones. Use a pattern where position 7 is charged (Glu).
  2. Fold your "protein" so all the hydrophobic beads are tucked inside and charged ones face outward toward the "water." This is how real proteins fold.
  3. Now swap bead 7 for a hydrophobic bead (Glu → Val). It now sits on the surface but "wants" to hide from water.
  4. Make a second model the same way and see how the two sticky surfaces could cling together. That's how sickle hemoglobin molecules chain into long rods.
