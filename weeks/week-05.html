DNA: Structure & Replication

Inside almost every one of your cells is about two meters of DNA carrying roughly 6 billion letters of code. This week we discover how scientists figured out its structure, and how a cell copies it with astonishing accuracy.

Learning objectives

  1. Summarize the key experiments showing that DNA is the genetic material.
  2. Describe the structure of DNA: nucleotides, base pairing, antiparallel strands, double helix.
  3. Apply Chargaff's rules and write complementary sequences.
  4. Explain how DNA is packaged into chromosomes and what a genome is.
  5. Describe semiconservative replication and the roles of helicase, primase, DNA polymerase and ligase.
  6. Explain leading vs. lagging strand synthesis and the role of telomeres.

1. The hunt for the genetic material

By the 1940s, scientists knew genes were on chromosomes, which are made of protein and DNA. Most bet on protein: with 20 amino acids it seemed far more complex than DNA's mere four bases. A series of clever experiments proved them wrong.

  • Griffith (1928) found that a harmless strain of pneumonia bacteria could be transformed into a deadly one by mixing it with heat-killed deadly bacteria. Some "transforming principle" was passing between them.
  • Avery, MacLeod and McCarty (1944) destroyed each type of molecule in turn. Destroying proteins or RNA didn't stop transformation, but destroying DNA did. DNA was the transforming principle.
  • Hershey and Chase (1952) labeled viruses that infect bacteria with radioactive tags: sulfur for protein, phosphorus for DNA. Only the DNA entered the bacteria to make new viruses. That settled it.

2. The double helix

In 1952, Rosalind Franklin and her student Raymond Gosling produced X-ray diffraction images of DNA, most famously Photo 51. Its X-shaped pattern revealed a helix with regular repeating dimensions. Combined with Chargaff's rules (in any DNA sample, %A = %T and %G = %C), James Watson and Francis Crick built their famous model in 1953. Watson, Crick and Maurice Wilkins received the Nobel Prize in 1962. Franklin had died of cancer in 1958 at age 37, and the prize is not awarded posthumously. Her essential role is now widely recognized.

The structure is elegant:

  • Each strand is a chain of nucleotides, each made of a deoxyribose sugar, a phosphate and one of four bases: Adenine, Thymine, Guanine, Cytosine.
  • The sugars and phosphates form the backbone on the outside, like the rails of a ladder. The bases point inward like rungs.
  • Bases pair by hydrogen bonds: A with T (2 bonds) and G with C (3 bonds). A double-ring purine (A, G) always pairs with a single-ring pyrimidine (T, C), keeping the ladder a uniform 2 nm wide. This pairing explains Chargaff's rules.
  • The two strands are antiparallel: one runs 5'→3', the other 3'→5'. (The numbers refer to carbon atoms in the sugar; the 5' end has a free phosphate and the 3' end a free –OH.)
  • The ladder twists into a right-handed double helix, completing one turn about every 10 base pairs.

The most important consequence of base pairing: each strand contains all the information needed to rebuild the other. If one strand reads 5'-ATGCCA-3', its partner must read 3'-TACGGT-5'. As Watson and Crick wrote with famous understatement, this "immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism."

A DNA sample is 22% cytosine. What are the percentages of the other bases?

G = C = 22%, so G + C = 44%. The remaining 56% splits equally: A = T = 28%.

3. Chromosomes, genes and genomes

If you stretched out the DNA from one human cell it would be about 2 meters long, yet it fits in a nucleus about 6 µm wide. That's like packing 40 km of thread into a tennis ball. The trick is packaging:

  1. DNA wraps around spools of proteins called histones, forming nucleosomes ("beads on a string").
  2. Nucleosomes coil into thicker fibers, which loop and fold.
  3. Before cell division, chromosomes condense further into the familiar X-shaped structures (two copied sister chromatids joined at a centromere).

Humans have 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs: 22 pairs of autosomes plus the sex chromosomes (XX or XY). One set of 23 comes from each parent. Your complete set of DNA is your genome, about 3.1 billion base pairs per set. A gene is a stretch of DNA that codes for a functional product, usually a protein. Humans have only about 20,000 protein-coding genes, making up less than 2% of the genome. Much of the rest regulates genes, provides structure, or is made of ancient viral and repetitive sequences whose functions are still being studied.

🧬 Human connection: the Human Genome Project

Launched in 1990 and declared essentially complete in 2003, the Human Genome Project cost about $3 billion to read one human genome. In 2022 the Telomere-to-Telomere consortium filled in the last gaps. Today a whole genome can be sequenced for a few hundred dollars in about a day, which is transforming medicine, ancestry research and forensics (Week 8).

4. DNA replication

Before a cell divides, it must copy all of its DNA, and do so accurately. Replication is semiconservative: the two strands separate, each serves as a template, and each new double helix contains one original strand and one new strand. Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl confirmed this in 1958 by growing bacteria in "heavy" nitrogen and tracking the DNA's density over generations.

The replication crew

EnzymeJob
HelicaseUnwinds and unzips the double helix, creating a replication fork
TopoisomeraseRelieves the tension from twisting ahead of the fork
PrimaseLays down a short RNA primer, because polymerase can't start a new strand from nothing
DNA polymeraseAdds complementary nucleotides to the 3' end of the growing strand, and proofreads
DNA ligaseSeals the gaps between DNA fragments

Leading and lagging strands

DNA polymerase has one strict rule: it can only build in the 5'→3' direction. Because the two template strands are antiparallel, this creates a problem at the fork:

  • The leading strand is built continuously, chasing the fork as it opens.
  • The lagging strand must be built away from the fork, in short pieces called Okazaki fragments, each started by its own primer. Ligase then stitches them together.

Replication starts at thousands of origins along each human chromosome at once, so the whole genome can be copied in a few hours. Proofreading and repair systems bring the error rate down to about one mistake per billion bases, a handful of new changes each time a cell divides. Those rare errors are mutations, the raw material of both disease and evolution (Weeks 6 and 8).

Telomeres: the end-replication problem

At the very tip of a linear chromosome, the lagging strand can't be completed, so chromosomes lose a little DNA with each division. Protective caps called telomeres (thousands of repeats of TTAGGG) act as buffers. In most body cells they shorten over a lifetime, and very short telomeres trigger cells to stop dividing, a factor in aging. Stem cells, and about 90% of cancers, use the enzyme telomerase to rebuild telomeres and keep dividing.

Key takeaways
  • Griffith, Avery and Hershey–Chase showed that DNA is the genetic material. Franklin's data and Watson & Crick's model revealed its structure.
  • DNA is a double helix of antiparallel strands with A–T and G–C base pairs.
  • Humans have 46 chromosomes and a genome of ~3.1 billion base pairs with ~20,000 genes.
  • Replication is semiconservative: helicase unzips, primase primes, polymerase builds 5'→3' and ligase seals.
  • The leading strand is built continuously and the lagging strand in Okazaki fragments. Telomeres protect chromosome ends.

Virtual Lab

Practical: Extract DNA from Strawberries

Strawberries are perfect for DNA extraction: they're soft, and they're octoploid, with 8 copies of each chromosome, so they're loaded with DNA. You'll break open the cells, strip away the proteins, and make the DNA visible to the naked eye.

⚠️ SafetyRubbing alcohol is flammable and toxic. Keep it away from flames and don't drink it. Work in a ventilated space and wash your hands after.

Materials

  • 2–3 strawberries (green tops removed)
  • Zip-top bag
  • Extraction buffer: ½ cup water + 2 tsp dish soap + ½ tsp salt, stirred gently
  • Coffee filter or paper towel, and a funnel or cup
  • Tall clear glass or test tube
  • Ice-cold rubbing alcohol (chilled in the freezer)
  • Wooden skewer or toothpick

Procedure

  1. Put the strawberries in the bag, seal it, and mash for 2 minutes. (Mechanically breaks cell walls.)
  2. Add 3 tablespoons of extraction buffer, reseal, and mash gently for 1 more minute without making too many bubbles. (Soap dissolves the membranes; salt helps the DNA clump.)
  3. Filter the mush through the coffee filter into the glass until you have about 2 cm of liquid.
  4. Tilt the glass and slowly pour cold alcohol down the side to form a layer about as deep as the strawberry liquid. Don't mix!
  5. Wait 1–2 minutes. White, cloudy, stringy material will appear at the boundary and float up. That's DNA, millions of strands clumped together.
  6. Twirl the skewer at the boundary to spool the DNA.
