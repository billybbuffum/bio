1. What does it mean to be alive?

Biology is the study of life, but "life" turns out to be surprisingly hard to define in a single sentence. Instead, biologists recognize life by a set of shared properties. Every living thing, from a bacterium to a blue whale to you:

  • Is made of one or more cells, the smallest units that can carry out life's processes.
  • Uses energy through metabolism, the chemical reactions that build and break down molecules.
  • Maintains homeostasis, keeping its internal conditions stable (your body holds itself near 37 °C).
  • Grows and develops following instructions encoded in DNA.
  • Reproduces and passes genetic information to offspring.
  • Responds to stimuli in its environment.
  • Evolves: populations change over generations.

Viruses are the classic edge case. They contain genetic material and evolve, but they cannot reproduce or metabolize outside a host cell, so most biologists don't consider them fully alive.

Biology is organized in levels, and this course climbs them: atoms → molecules → organelles → cells → tissues → organs → organ systems → organisms → populations → ecosystems. At each level new properties emerge that aren't present in the parts alone. A single water molecule isn't wet, and a single neuron can't think.

2. How science works

Science is a method for finding out what's true while guarding against fooling ourselves. A typical investigation looks like this:

  1. Observation: noticing something that needs explaining.
  2. Question: "Why does this happen?"
  3. Hypothesis: a testable and falsifiable explanation, often phrased as "If … then …".
  4. Experiment: a fair test that changes one thing at a time.
  5. Analysis: do the data support or refute the hypothesis?
  6. Communication & replication: other scientists repeat the work to see if it holds up.

A fair experiment has three kinds of variables:

  • The independent variable is the one thing you change on purpose.
  • The dependent variable is what you measure to see the effect.
  • Controlled variables are everything else, which you keep the same so they can't explain your result.

A control group receives no treatment (or a placebo) and gives you a baseline. Without one, you can't tell whether your treatment did anything at all.

🫀 Human connection: the doctor who said "wash your hands"

In 1847, Ignaz Semmelweis noticed that women in one Vienna maternity ward died of fever far more often than in another. The difference was that doctors in the deadly ward came straight from performing autopsies. He hypothesized that "cadaverous particles" on their hands caused the disease, and required handwashing with chlorinated lime. Deaths dropped dramatically. Germ theory explained why only decades later, but his controlled comparison saved lives first.

Sample size matters. Living things vary. If you test one plant per group, a single unusually healthy plant could fool you. Larger samples and repeated trials let real effects stand out from random noise. You'll see this for yourself in the Virtual Lab.

Hypothesis vs. theory. In everyday speech, "it's just a theory" means "it's a guess." In science, a theory is the opposite: a broad explanation supported by an enormous body of evidence from many independent lines of investigation. Cell theory, germ theory, the theory of evolution and atomic theory are among the best-supported ideas humans have.

A study finds that people who drink more coffee live longer. Does coffee cause longer life?

Not necessarily! This is a correlation. Coffee drinkers might differ in other ways, such as income or health habits. These are confounding variables. Only a controlled experiment, or very careful statistical analysis, can establish causation.

3. Atoms: the elements of life

All matter is made of atoms. Each atom has a tiny nucleus containing positively charged protons and neutral neutrons, surrounded by a cloud of negatively charged electrons. The number of protons defines the element: carbon always has 6, oxygen always has 8.

Atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons are isotopes. Carbon-12 (6 neutrons) is stable, while carbon-14 (8 neutrons) is radioactive and decays at a steady rate, which lets scientists date ancient remains. In medicine, radioactive isotopes power PET scans and some cancer treatments.

Just six elements, often remembered as CHNOPS, make up about 98% of your body mass:

Element% of body massWhere you find it
Oxygen (O)~65%Water, every biomolecule
Carbon (C)~18%The backbone of all organic molecules
Hydrogen (H)~10%Water, organic molecules
Nitrogen (N)~3%Proteins, DNA
Calcium (Ca)~1.5%Bones, teeth, muscle and nerve signaling
Phosphorus (P)~1%DNA, ATP, bones, cell membranes

Carbon is special because it can form four strong bonds, letting it build chains, rings and branches. That versatility is why life on Earth is carbon-based.

4. Chemical bonds

Atoms bond to fill their outer electron shells. Three kinds of bond matter most in biology:

  • Covalent bonds form when atoms share electrons. They are strong and hold molecules together. When the sharing is unequal, the bond is polar and each end carries a partial charge (δ⁺ or δ⁻).
  • Ionic bonds form when one atom transfers electrons to another, creating oppositely charged ions that attract. Table salt (Na⁺Cl⁻) is the classic example. Ions such as Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻ carry the electrical signals in your nerves and heart.
  • Hydrogen bonds are weak attractions between a δ⁺ hydrogen and a δ⁻ atom (usually O or N) on another molecule. Each one is weak, but millions together are strong. They hold the two strands of DNA together and fold proteins into shape.

5. Water: the molecule of life

You are about 60% water by weight. Life began in water, and every chemical reaction in your cells happens in it. Water's unusual properties all come from its polarity and the hydrogen bonds it forms:

  • Cohesion and adhesion. Water sticks to itself (cohesion, which creates surface tension) and to other surfaces (adhesion). Together they pull water up thin tubes, from roots to the tops of trees.
  • High specific heat. Water absorbs a lot of heat before its temperature rises. Oceans moderate climate, and your watery body resists sudden temperature swings.
  • Evaporative cooling. Evaporating water carries a lot of heat away. That's why sweating cools you.
  • Ice floats. Hydrogen bonds lock ice into an open lattice that is less dense than liquid water. Lakes freeze from the top down, and the life below survives.
  • The universal solvent. Water dissolves ionic and polar substances (sugars, salts, amino acids). Blood plasma is ~92% water and carries dissolved nutrients, hormones and wastes around your body. Molecules that dissolve in water are hydrophilic. Nonpolar molecules like oils are hydrophobic and clump together, a fact that will explain cell membranes in Week 3.
Why does a sweaty day feel worse when it's humid?

In humid air, sweat evaporates slowly, so less heat leaves your skin. Evaporation, not sweating itself, is what cools you.

6. Acids, bases and pH

A tiny fraction of water molecules split into ions: H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻. An acid adds H⁺ ions to a solution; a base removes them (or adds OH⁻). The pH scale measures H⁺ concentration:

pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]

The scale runs from 0 to 14. Pure water is neutral at pH 7, acids are below 7, and bases are above. Because the scale is logarithmic, each step is a 10-fold change: pH 3 has 10 times more H⁺ than pH 4 and 100 times more than pH 5.

Your body runs on precise pH values. Stomach acid sits near pH 1.5–2 to kill microbes and activate digestive enzymes. The small intestine is slightly basic (~pH 8), and blood is held between 7.35 and 7.45. Below that range is acidosis and above it is alkalosis; both can be fatal because proteins change shape when pH shifts.

What keeps blood so steady? Buffers, chemicals that soak up excess H⁺ or release it when needed. The main blood buffer is the carbonic acid–bicarbonate system:

CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻

Your lungs and kidneys tune this system constantly. Breathe faster and you blow off CO₂, which pulls the reaction left and raises pH. This links chemistry directly to your respiratory system (Week 10).

Key takeaways
  • Life is recognized by a set of properties: cells, metabolism, homeostasis, growth, reproduction, response and evolution.
  • Good experiments change one variable, measure another, control the rest, include a control group, and use enough samples.
  • Life is mostly C, H, N, O, P and S, held together by covalent, ionic and hydrogen bonds.
  • Water's polarity gives it cohesion, high specific heat, evaporative cooling, floating ice and solvent power.
  • pH is logarithmic, and buffers keep blood at pH ~7.4.

Practical: Make a Red-Cabbage pH Indicator

Red cabbage contains anthocyanin pigments that change shape, and therefore color, depending on pH. You'll extract them and use them to test the acidity of things in your kitchen.

⚠️ Safety Have an adult nearby when using boiling water. Never mix bleach with vinegar or ammonia, because it releases toxic gas; test bleach only on its own, or skip it. Don't taste anything. Wash your hands afterward.

Materials

  • ¼ red cabbage, chopped
  • Hot water (about 2 cups) and a pot, kettle or heat-proof bowl
  • Strainer
  • 6–10 clear cups or glasses, or a white ice-cube tray
  • Spoon, marker and labels
  • Test substances: vinegar, lemon juice, baking soda dissolved in water, tap water, milk, dish soap, a soda, antacid tablet in water, laundry detergent in water

Procedure

  1. Pour hot (not quite boiling) water over the chopped cabbage. Let it steep 10–15 min until the water is deep purple.
  2. Strain the liquid. This is your indicator. Let it cool.
  3. Pour equal amounts (about 3 tablespoons) into each cup, and label the cups.
  4. Before testing, write a prediction for each substance: acid, neutral or base?
  5. Add 1 spoonful of each substance to its own cup. Keep one cup of plain indicator as your control.
  6. Record the color, then use the color chart in the Virtual Lab (red-cabbage swatch) to estimate pH.
  7. Bonus: to a cup that turned pink from vinegar, slowly add baking-soda solution. What happens?
Red-cabbage color key (approximate): red (pH 1–2) · pink/magenta (3–4) · purple (5–7) · blue (8) · blue-green (9–10) · green (11–12) · yellow (13–14)
