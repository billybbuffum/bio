Cell Division & Inheritance

How does one fertilized egg become 30 trillion cells? And why do you look like your parents, but not exactly? This week connects the movement of chromosomes to the patterns of inheritance Gregor Mendel discovered in his pea garden.

1. The cell cycle

Your body makes millions of new cells every second, replacing skin, gut lining and blood. Dividing cells follow the cell cycle:

  • Interphase (most of the cycle): G1 (growth and normal work), S (DNA synthesis, when each chromosome is copied into two identical sister chromatids), and G2 (preparing to divide).
  • M phase: mitosis (dividing the nucleus) followed by cytokinesis (dividing the cytoplasm).

Some cells, like most neurons and heart muscle cells, exit the cycle into a resting state called G0 and rarely divide again. Molecular checkpoints act like quality-control inspectors: is the DNA undamaged? Fully copied? Are all chromosomes attached to the spindle? The protein p53, the "guardian of the genome," halts the cycle or triggers cell suicide (apoptosis) when DNA is damaged.

🫀 Human connection: cancer is cell division out of control

Cancer develops when mutations accumulate in genes that control the cell cycle. Damaged "accelerators" (oncogenes) get stuck on, and damaged "brakes" (tumor suppressors such as p53 and BRCA1) fail. About half of all human cancers carry a mutated p53. Many chemotherapy drugs target rapidly dividing cells, which is why they also affect hair follicles and the gut lining.

2. Mitosis: making identical copies

Mitosis produces two genetically identical diploid daughter cells. It is used for growth, repair and asexual reproduction. Remember the phases with PMAT:

  1. Prophase: chromosomes condense into visible X shapes, the nuclear envelope breaks down, and the spindle (made of microtubules) begins to form.
  2. Metaphase: chromosomes line up single-file along the cell's middle.
  3. Anaphase: sister chromatids are pulled apart toward opposite poles.
  4. Telophase: two new nuclei form, and cytokinesis splits the cell in two.

3. Meiosis: making unique gametes

Your body cells are diploid (2n = 46): two sets of 23 chromosomes, one from each parent. Each pair consists of homologous chromosomes carrying the same genes, though possibly different versions. If eggs and sperm were also diploid, the chromosome number would double every generation. Meiosis solves this by producing haploid (n = 23) gametes through two divisions:

  • Meiosis I separates homologous pairs. In prophase I, homologs pair up and exchange segments (crossing over). In metaphase I, pairs line up side by side, each pair oriented randomly.
  • Meiosis II separates sister chromatids, much like mitosis.

The result: four genetically unique haploid cells. Three sources of variation make you unique:

  1. Independent assortment: with 23 pairs, each parent can make 2²³ ≈ 8.4 million chromosome combinations.
  2. Crossing over shuffles alleles within chromosomes, creating essentially unlimited combinations.
  3. Random fertilization: any sperm × any egg gives about 70 trillion combinations before crossing over is even counted.

When chromosomes fail to separate properly (nondisjunction), gametes end up with an extra or missing chromosome. Trisomy 21 (three copies of chromosome 21) causes Down syndrome. Its risk rises with maternal age, partly because eggs pause partway through meiosis I for decades.

4. Mendel and the laws of inheritance

In the 1850s–60s, the monk Gregor Mendel bred about 28,000 pea plants and counted their traits. Before him, most people believed in "blending inheritance," where offspring are an average of their parents. Mendel showed that traits are passed on as discrete units, which we now call genes, and he did this without knowing DNA or chromosomes existed.

When he crossed pure purple-flowered plants with pure white ones, all offspring (F1) were purple. When the F1 plants self-pollinated, white reappeared in the F2 generation in a ratio of about 3 purple : 1 white (705:224 in his data). The white "factor" had been hidden, not lost.

Vocabulary

  • A gene comes in versions called alleles (e.g., A = purple, a = white).
  • Genotype is the alleles you carry (AA, Aa, aa). Phenotype is the trait you show (purple, white).
  • Homozygous means two identical alleles (AA or aa). Heterozygous means two different alleles (Aa).
  • A dominant allele shows its effect even with one copy. A recessive allele shows only when there is no dominant allele.

Mendel's laws

  1. Law of segregation: the two alleles for a gene separate during gamete formation, so each gamete carries one. (This is homologs separating in meiosis I.)
  2. Law of independent assortment: alleles for different genes sort independently. (This is random orientation in metaphase I.) It applies to genes on different chromosomes. Genes close together on the same chromosome are linked.

Punnett squares

A Punnett square lists one parent's possible gametes across the top and the other's down the side; each box is a possible offspring. For Aa × Aa:

AAAAa
aAaaa

Genotypes come out 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa, and phenotypes 3 dominant : 1 recessive. These are probabilities for each child, not guarantees. A couple who are both carriers has a 25% chance of an affected child with every pregnancy.

A purple pea plant could be AA or Aa. How could you find out which?

Do a test cross: cross it with a white (aa) plant. If any offspring are white, the purple parent must be Aa. If all of many offspring are purple, it's almost certainly AA.

5. Beyond Mendel

  • Incomplete dominance: heterozygotes are intermediate, as when red × white snapdragons give pink.
  • Codominance: both alleles show fully. ABO blood type is the classic case: IA and IB are codominant and both dominant over i.
  • Multiple alleles: a gene can have more than two alleles in a population (ABO has three).
  • Polygenic traits: height, skin color and eye color are shaped by many genes, giving smooth bell-curve variation. Even "simple" textbook traits like eye color and tongue rolling turn out not to follow single-gene patterns in real people.
  • Environment matters: genes set a range, and nutrition, health and experience act within it.
  • Sex-linked traits: genes on the X chromosome show a special pattern. Females are XX and males XY, so a male with one recessive X allele shows the trait. Red-green color blindness affects ~8% of men of Northern European ancestry but only ~0.5% of women. Hemophilia, famously passed through Queen Victoria's descendants, is another example.

6. Pedigrees: inheritance in families

We can't run breeding experiments on humans, so geneticists study family trees called pedigrees. Squares are males and circles are females; shaded symbols are affected individuals; horizontal lines join parents, and vertical lines lead to children.

  • Autosomal recessive (e.g., cystic fibrosis, sickle-cell disease): can skip generations, and unaffected carrier parents can have affected children.
  • Autosomal dominant (e.g., Huntington's disease, Marfan syndrome): appears in every generation, and every affected person has an affected parent.
  • X-linked recessive (e.g., hemophilia, color blindness): mostly affects males, who inherit it through carrier mothers.
Key takeaways
  • Cell cycle: G1 → S (DNA copied) → G2 → mitosis → cytokinesis, policed by checkpoints. When these fail, cancer can result.
  • Mitosis gives 2 identical diploid cells. Meiosis gives 4 unique haploid gametes.
  • Variation comes from independent assortment, crossing over and random fertilization.
  • Mendel's laws of segregation and independent assortment, worked out with Punnett squares, predict probabilities.
  • Real inheritance also includes incomplete dominance, codominance, polygenic traits, sex linkage and environment.

Practical: Coin-Flip Genetics

Meiosis is a coin toss: each parent passes on one of their two alleles at random. You'll use coins as gametes to (A) "make a baby" of a fictional species and (B) test Mendel's 3:1 ratio with real chance.

NoteThe traits in Part A belong to an imaginary creature, the "Biobeast," and follow simple one-gene rules to make the mechanics clear. Real human facial features are polygenic.

Part A: Make a Biobeast

Both parents are heterozygous for every trait. For each trait, flip coin 1 for mom and coin 2 for dad: heads = dominant allele (capital), tails = recessive allele (lowercase). Record the genotype and phenotype, then draw your Biobeast!

Part B: Testing the 3:1 ratio

Flip both coins together 40 times (a cross of Tt × Tt). Tally each outcome: HH = TT, HT or TH = Tt, TT = tt.

Expected: TT = 10, Tt = 20, tt = 10, so 30 curly : 10 straight. Use the Punnett lab's chi-square feature (set N = 40) to compare, or compute χ² = Σ (O − E)² / E yourself. The critical value for 1 degree of freedom is 3.84.
