1. The cardiovascular system

Your heart beats about 100,000 times a day, pumping roughly 7,000 liters of blood through some 100,000 km of blood vessels. That's enough to circle Earth twice.

The heart: a double pump

The human heart has four chambers: two atria (receiving chambers) on top and two ventricles (pumping chambers) below, separated into right and left sides by a wall called the septum. It drives two circuits:

  • Pulmonary circuit: right side → lungs → back to the left side. Blood picks up O₂ and drops off CO₂.
  • Systemic circuit: left side → whole body → back to the right side. The left ventricle has the thickest wall because it pumps against the highest pressure.

Path: body → vena cava → right atrium → tricuspid valve → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary veins → left atrium → mitral valve → left ventricle → aorta → body. Valves keep flow one-way, and the "lub-dub" of your heartbeat is the sound of valves snapping shut. The heartbeat starts in the heart itself, in the sinoatrial (SA) node, the natural pacemaker. Nerves and hormones only speed it up or slow it down.

Blood vessels

  • Arteries carry blood away from the heart. They have thick, elastic, muscular walls to handle high pressure, and that pressure surge is your pulse.
  • Capillaries have walls only one cell thick, where exchange with tissues happens. No cell is more than a few cells away from one.
  • Veins return blood to the heart at low pressure, helped by one-way valves and squeezing skeletal muscles.

Blood pressure is written as systolic over diastolic (ventricles contracting / relaxing), with about 120/80 mmHg typical for a healthy adult. Chronic high blood pressure strains the heart and damages vessels.

Blood types are set by antigens on red cells. Type A has A antigens and anti-B antibodies in plasma; type B the reverse; AB has both antigens and neither antibody; O has neither antigen and both antibodies. The Rh(D) antigen adds + or −. A mismatched transfusion makes the recipient's antibodies clump (agglutinate) and destroy donor cells, which can be fatal. You'll practice this in Lab 10B.

🫀 Human connection: heart disease

Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death worldwide. In atherosclerosis, fatty plaques build up in artery walls. If a plaque ruptures in a coronary artery, a clot can block blood to part of the heart muscle (a heart attack). In a brain artery, it causes a stroke. Risk factors include high LDL cholesterol, high blood pressure, smoking, diabetes, inactivity and genetics. Regular exercise strengthens the heart, lowering resting heart rate, which you'll measure in this week's practical.

2. The respiratory system

Air flows through the nose → pharynx → larynx → trachea → two bronchi → ever-smaller bronchioles → about 480 million alveoli, tiny air sacs wrapped in capillaries. Together they provide a gas-exchange surface of roughly 70 m², about the size of a badminton court. O₂ diffuses from the alveoli into the blood and CO₂ diffuses out, because each moves down its concentration gradient across walls just ~0.5 µm thick.

Breathing is driven by the diaphragm. When it contracts and flattens, along with the rib muscles, the chest expands, pressure drops and air rushes in. Exhaling at rest is passive. Surprisingly, your urge to breathe is triggered mainly by rising CO₂ (which makes blood more acidic) sensed in the brainstem, not by low O₂. That links back to the bicarbonate buffer from Week 1.

About 98% of O₂ in blood rides on hemoglobin. Carbon monoxide binds hemoglobin ~200 times more tightly than O₂, which is why CO detectors save lives. Cigarette smoke paralyzes the cilia that sweep mucus from the airways and, over time, destroys alveoli (emphysema).

3. The digestive system

Digestion breaks food into molecules small enough to absorb, using mechanical (chewing, churning) and chemical (enzymes, using hydrolysis from Week 2) processes.

  • Mouth: teeth grind, and salivary amylase begins starch digestion.
  • Esophagus: waves of smooth-muscle contraction (peristalsis) push food down, even upside down.
  • Stomach: acid (pH ~2) kills microbes and denatures proteins, and pepsin starts protein digestion. A mucus layer protects the stomach wall.
  • Small intestine (~6 m): the main site of digestion and absorption. The pancreas adds enzymes (amylase, trypsin, lipase) and bicarbonate to neutralize the acid. The liver makes bile, stored in the gallbladder, which emulsifies fats. Folds, villi and microvilli create a huge absorptive surface. Sugars and amino acids enter the blood and go first to the liver; fats enter the lymph.
  • Large intestine: absorbs water and hosts trillions of bacteria (the microbiome) that ferment fiber, make some vitamins and influence immunity.

The liver is a chemical processing plant. It stores glycogen, detoxifies drugs and alcohol (using smooth ER from Week 3), makes blood proteins and bile, and converts ammonia into urea.

4. The urinary system

Your two kidneys filter your entire blood plasma about 60 times a day, around 180 liters, yet produce only ~1.5 liters of urine. Each contains about a million nephrons:

  1. Filtration: blood pressure forces water and small molecules out of a capillary knot (the glomerulus) into the nephron. Cells and large proteins stay behind.
  2. Reabsorption: about 99% of the water, plus all the glucose and amino acids and most salts, are pulled back into the blood.
  3. Secretion: extra wastes, drugs and H⁺ are actively pumped into the urine.

The kidneys are homeostasis champions. They control water balance (antidiuretic hormone, ADH, makes you conserve water when dehydrated), blood pressure, pH and salt levels. Glucose in urine is a classic sign of diabetes: blood glucose is so high that the nephrons can't reabsorb it all.

5. The immune system

You're under constant attack by bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites. Your immune system defends in layers.

Innate immunity: fast and general

  • Barriers: skin, mucus, cilia, stomach acid, tears and saliva (containing the enzyme lysozyme), and friendly microbes that crowd out pathogens.
  • Inflammation: damaged tissue releases histamine. Blood vessels widen and leak, causing redness, heat, swelling and pain, and bringing in defenders.
  • Phagocytes: neutrophils and macrophages engulf and digest invaders (phagocytosis + lysosomes, Week 3).
  • Natural killer cells destroy virus-infected and cancerous cells. Fever slows microbial growth and speeds immune responses.

Adaptive immunity: specific, with memory

Adaptive immunity recognizes specific molecules called antigens. It relies on lymphocytes made in the bone marrow, each born with a unique receptor shaped by genetic shuffling of receptor genes. Together they can recognize billions of different shapes.

  • Helper T cells (CD4⁺) are the coordinators. When shown an antigen by a macrophage or dendritic cell, they activate B cells and cytotoxic T cells. HIV destroys helper T cells, which is why untreated HIV leads to AIDS.
  • B cells become plasma cells that pour out antibodies, Y-shaped proteins that bind specific antigens. Antibodies neutralize toxins and viruses and tag microbes for destruction.
  • Cytotoxic T cells (CD8⁺) kill your own cells that are infected or cancerous.
  • Memory B and T cells persist for years or decades, launching a faster, stronger secondary response on re-exposure. That's why you usually get chickenpox only once.

Vaccines and herd immunity

A vaccine presents a harmless antigen (an inactivated or weakened microbe, a protein piece, or mRNA instructions to make one) so your body forms memory cells without suffering the disease. Vaccines eradicated smallpox, which killed an estimated 300 million people in the 20th century alone, and have nearly eliminated polio. When enough people are immune, an infected person meets too few susceptible people to keep the chain going. This is herd immunity, and it protects newborns and people who can't be vaccinated. The more contagious the disease (the higher its R₀), the higher the coverage needed: ~95% for measles.

🫀 Human connection: when immunity misfires

Allergies are overreactions to harmless antigens like pollen or peanuts, driven by IgE antibodies and histamine. Antihistamines block the histamine. Autoimmune diseases happen when the immune system attacks the body's own cells: type 1 diabetes (beta cells), multiple sclerosis (myelin), rheumatoid arthritis (joints). New cancer immunotherapies release the brakes on T cells or engineer them (CAR-T cells) to attack tumors.

Key takeaways
  • The heart is a four-chambered double pump. Arteries carry blood away from the heart, veins return it, and capillaries exchange.
  • Red cells carry O₂ on hemoglobin. ABO/Rh antigens determine transfusion compatibility.
  • Alveoli exchange gases by diffusion, and CO₂ level drives breathing.
  • Digestion uses enzymes (hydrolysis). The small intestine absorbs, and the large intestine reclaims water and houses the microbiome.
  • Nephrons filter, reabsorb and secrete to maintain water, salt and pH balance.
  • Innate immunity is fast and general. Adaptive immunity (B cells, antibodies, T cells) is specific and has memory, which vaccines exploit.

Virtual Lab

Practical: Heart Rate, Exercise & Lung Capacity

⚠️ SafetyOnly exercise at a level that's comfortable for you. Skip the exercise part if you have a heart or breathing condition or feel unwell; you can use a friend's data instead. Stop if you feel dizzy or have chest pain.

Part A: Heart rate and recovery

  1. Find your pulse on your wrist (radial artery, thumb side) or neck (carotid artery) with two fingers, not your thumb.
  2. Sit quietly for 3 minutes. Count beats for 15 seconds and multiply by 4. This is your resting heart rate (minute 0).
  3. Do 1 minute of jumping jacks or stair climbing.
  4. Immediately measure your heart rate (minute 1), then again every minute for 5 more minutes while sitting.
  5. Optional: repeat on another day, or compare with a family member.

Part B: Estimate your lung capacity

  1. Take the deepest breath you can, then blow out as much air as possible into a round balloon in one breath. Pinch it closed.
  2. Measure the balloon's widest circumference C (cm) with string. Diameter d = C ÷ π. Volume ≈ (π/6) × d³ cm³. Divide by 1000 for liters.
  3. Repeat 3 times and average. This approximates your vital capacity (typical adults: 3–5 L).
